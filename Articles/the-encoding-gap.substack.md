# The encoding gap

*Doing work has always produced two things. Capable assistants have made one of them optional.*

[ IMAGE — hero, Metaphor: a researcher's chair from behind, monitor visible with a completed piece of work on screen, but the chair is faintly empty; or an abstract of two overlapping vessels, one filled and one hollow. The concept: the output is there, the person is not. Sits above the byline. ]

A few years ago at a previous company, I ran a study fast. The PR team needed data by end of week for an opportunity that couldn't wait. It went on top of a list I was already behind on, so I leaned heavily on ChatGPT for the analyses. The study was methodologically sound. The results were correct. When it landed, colleagues asked me to walk them through it, and I couldn't do it the way I usually could. I knew what the data said. I couldn't tell them how I knew.

As a researcher, it felt wrong.

I've been thinking about that gap ever since. Not because the study was flawed. It wasn't. Because the gap between having done the work and being able to speak to the work was new. And it's the gap I want to write about.

## What doing work has always given us besides the output

There are two things that come out of any piece of knowledge work. One is the artefact: the deck, the report, the code, the slide. The other is what happens to the person who did it. Working through a problem shapes what you can retrieve later, what you can explain, and what you can build on. That second output has always been less visible than the first. It's what makes a body of work compound over time.

Fifty years of memory research has been circling this. In 1978, Norman Slamecka and Peter Graf ran [a series of experiments](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592) now known as the "generation effect": people who produced words themselves remembered them better than people who read the same words, even when both groups spent the same time on the task. The effort of generating created a stronger memory trace than the identical input passively received.

Two decades later, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke took the finding further. In [a 2006 paper in *Perspectives on Psychological Science*](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2006.00012.x), they showed that retrieving information, even trying to retrieve it and getting it wrong, strengthens memory more than restudying the same information. Testing yourself isn't just measurement. It's a memory event. The act of pulling something back out of your head consolidates it in a way that reading it again does not.

Before either of those, Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart's 1972 "levels of processing" framework had already established the pattern: the deeper you process information at the moment of encoding, semantically, connectively, effortfully, the more durable it becomes. Reading a sentence is shallow. Explaining why the sentence is true is deep. The output looks the same either way. What you keep is different.

The consistent picture across five decades of research: what you retain from a task tracks the generative effort you invested, not the answer you left with.

## Why the "we've always used tools" objection undersells the change

The obvious objection to any argument about knowledge work degrading in the age of capable assistants is that people have always used tools. Books, notebooks, calculators, spreadsheets, Wikipedia, spellcheck: none of these produced a generation of cognitively hollow adults. Daniel Wegner made this point formally in 1987 with the concept of transactive memory. We are, and have always been, systems of distributed cognition.

Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu, and Wegner published [the sharpest empirical version of the argument](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1207745) in *Science* in 2011. Once Google gave people confident, on-demand access to information, they remembered less of the information and more about where to find it. That was not a degradation. It was an adaptation. The mind reshaped itself around a new external memory system, and reshaped efficiently.

That adaptation is real, and it complicates any easy story about assistants stripping us of memory. It's also where the argument turns. The tools Sparrow studied still required retrieval effort at the moment of use. Even a Google-primed adult has to formulate a query, scan results, evaluate, and integrate. A book requires you to open it and read. A notebook requires you to have written the note in the first place, and then to reread it. The efficiency sits in the storage. The generative work at the moment of use is still yours.

Capable assistants are the first widely used tools that break that pattern. The generative work at the moment of use is now shared, or in many cases handed over. This is the change worth naming, and 2025 has given us three studies that show what happens when you do it.

In June, [a team led by Nataliya Kosmyna at the MIT Media Lab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872) published a preprint using EEG to compare essay-writing across assistant-aided, search-engine and no-tool conditions. The assisted group showed the weakest neural connectivity across brain regions associated with memory, understanding, and self-reference. Participants who started sessions with the assistant and were later asked to write without it "struggled to regain their independence." The effect was persistent, not just a within-session behaviour. The paper isn't yet peer-reviewed, so hold that caveat. It's also methodologically the tightest study we have on the question.

The same year, [André Barcaui published a randomised controlled trial](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291125010186) in *Social Sciences & Humanities Open*. One hundred and twenty business students prepared for a topic using either ChatGPT or traditional study materials. Forty-five days later, the traditional-study group scored 68.5 per cent on a retention test. The ChatGPT group scored 57.5 per cent, despite spending less time on the assignment. And in a [666-participant mixed-methods study](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/15/1/6) in *Societies*, Michael Gerlich reported a strong negative correlation between reliance on these tools and critical-thinking scores, mediated by measured cognitive offloading.

## What breaks when the encoding doesn't happen

Come back to the study I ran fast for the PR opportunity. The results were correct. I could describe what the data said. What I couldn't do was speak to the analyses. I couldn't tell you how I got from the raw output to the framing. I couldn't defend the choices as though they were mine. The finished thing looked like something I'd made. Some part of my head knew it wasn't.

Susan Barnett and Stephen Ceci called this the transfer problem in [a 2002 review in *Psychological Bulletin*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12081085/). Being able to produce a correct answer on a task and being able to apply that understanding to a new task turn out to be two different things. Transfer requires internalised representation. It requires you to have built the object in your head, not just to have observed it come out of the pipeline. When the object isn't there internally, the transfer doesn't happen.

That framing lets you see the costs clearly. There are four, and they arrive at different points.

You can't build on the work. Six months later, when a related problem shows up, you can't start where the old work left off. You're starting from the artefact, not from the reasoning. Your future work doesn't compound with your past work the way it should.

You can't teach it. A junior colleague asks how you arrived at the framing, and you find yourself paraphrasing the artefact back to them. If they push you on why, you can't get to a first principle. You outsourced the first principles.

You can't defend it. Someone challenges a decision in a meeting. You're stuck between saying "the analysis showed" and admitting you can't fully reconstruct how the analysis got there.

You can't compound the expertise. Ten studies done this way don't produce the same practitioner as ten studies you did yourself. The gap widens. You wouldn't notice it in the first year. You'd notice it in the fifth.

These costs are all delayed. That's the interesting thing about them, and the dangerous thing.

## What the practice looks like

The response isn't to stop using capable assistants. The productivity gain is real, and the option to work without them for anything substantial is no longer available in any workplace I know. The response is to notice that they've changed a piece of the workflow that used to happen automatically, and to build back the generative moments deliberately.

Two practices seem to do most of the work, and both are grounded in the memory research above. I'll write the full argument in a follow-up piece after I've run them on myself for a month.

The first is a sixty-second commit before opening the assistant. Write your first-pass answer or hypothesis. Even a wrong one. The point isn't to be right. The point is to activate the generation effect, and to create a prior state that the assistant's response then binds to rather than replaces.

The second is a closed-tab recall after the session. Close the tab. From memory, write three decisions you made, why for each, and what you rejected. Then reopen and check. That closed-tab recall is a testing-effect event: retrieval strengthens the trace, and the check calibrates your confidence.

Sixty seconds before, five minutes after. Roughly six minutes per session to buy back what an unassisted version of you would've got for free.

## What I'm turning over next

Starting today, I'm running these two practices on myself for a month, keeping a paper log of what worked, what felt like theatre, and what I skipped and why. In three to four weeks, I'll write the second piece in this series from that log. If the two hacks are enough, they're the answer. If they aren't, we'll find out what a second layer looks like together.

Between now and then, I'll be posting shorter observations on this on Notes, tracking the effect as it shows up. If you're running your own version of this experiment, formal or not, I'd genuinely like to hear about it. Reply to this email, or find me in Notes. Some of the best refinements to this practice will come from readers who tried it in a domain I don't work in.

There's a reason UX researchers see this problem before other knowledge workers do. We're trained to notice the gap between what a person can complete and what they can articulate. The most reliable finding in a moderated study is that a participant can succeed on a task and fail to explain why. We've spent careers watching that. It shouldn't surprise us to notice it in ourselves.

What's different this time is that the participant is us, and the task is our own work.
